# Coccidiosis

> ℹ️ **Nigeria context.** Use only NAFDAC-registered products; confirm the exact drug, dose and egg/meat withdrawal period with the product label and a licensed vet. Vaccinate per the National/State programme. Report notifiable diseases (avian influenza, Newcastle) to government veterinary services. NAFDAC bans chloramphenicol, nitrofurans/furazolidone, metronidazole and similar in food poultry — never use them.

**Cause:** Protozoan parasites of the genus *Eimeria* that damage the intestinal lining.

**Species/age:** Very common in young chickens, typically 3–8 weeks old, especially on damp, warm litter. Also affects other poultry.

**Key symptoms:**
- Bloody or dark, mucoid droppings (a classic sign with some Eimeria species).
- Pale comb, ruffled feathers, huddling, reluctance to move.
- Reduced feed/water intake, weight loss, poor growth.
- Variable mortality, higher in young birds.

**Transmission:** Ingestion of sporulated oocysts from contaminated litter, feed, or water. Wet litter dramatically increases risk.

**Treatment:** Anticoccidial medication is the mainstay. **Amprolium** is a widely used anticoccidial given in drinking water. **Toltrazuril** and **diclazuril** are also widely used anticoccidials in Nigeria. Sulphonamide anticoccidials (e.g. **sulphaquinoxaline**) are used as well. Supportive care: keep birds warm, provide vitamins (especially vitamin K and A) and electrolytes. A veterinarian should confirm the product, dose and withdrawal period.

**Confirmatory test:** Faecal oocyst count (e.g. McMaster) and/or post-mortem showing characteristic intestinal/caecal lesions with oocysts on a gut-mucosa scraping. A vet lab can confirm and identify the Eimeria species.

**Isolation/biosecurity:** Not strongly contagious bird-to-bird in the short term, but reduce spread by keeping litter dry, removing wet bedding, and avoiding overcrowding.

**Prevention:** Dry litter, good ventilation, in-feed coccidiostats or coccidiosis vaccination programmes, avoid overcrowding, clean waterers.

**When to call a vet:** Soon — if mortality is rising, droppings are heavily bloody, or treatment does not improve the flock within a couple of days.
